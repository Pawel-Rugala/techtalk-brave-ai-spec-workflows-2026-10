00 · Starting point

From sickle to a fleet of combines

Photos: Crop Trust (CC BY-SA 4.0) · W. van de Poll / Nationaal Archief (CC0) · D. Kollmann (CC BY-SA 3.0) · skfo.gov.ru (CC BY 4.0) — Wikimedia Commons

[1:30–3:00] A productivity leap like this has happened before. Take the harvest.

Sickle and scythe: one person, a fraction of a hectare a day. Every stalk goes through their hands. That's writing code by hand.

Reaper and thresher: the machine does the heavy lifting, but a human guides every step. An order of magnitude more output. That's an AI assistant — and that's where most teams are today.

Combine: one machine cuts, threshes and cleans. The human steers, but doesn't do the work anymore. Another order of magnitude. That's an AI agent.

Fleet of combines: one operator oversees several machines at once, and they follow planned routes on their own. That's an AI factory — and that's where we're heading.

Key point: none of these leaps came from the farmer swinging the scythe faster. Each time, the way of working changed — and each time there was more to learn. Anyone can swing a scythe; running a fleet of combines takes skill. Same with AI: anyone can use an assistant, but a factory has to be designed.

→ Transition: “So what does this shift look like in our work? Let's see where our effort goes today.”

[3:00–4:15]

Each bar is the team's total effort on a single feature. The proportions are illustrative — it's the direction that matters, not the percentages.

Before AI: coding was the biggest chunk. That's where the time went, and that's what we paid seniors for.

Today: the agent writes the code, so that chunk shrinks. The effort shifts two ways. BEFORE the code: intent, spec, architecture — in other words, thinking. And AFTER the code: review and verification — in other words, checking.

Punchline at the bottom of the slide: effort has shifted from writing code to writing intent and verification.
